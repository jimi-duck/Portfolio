Enter

I helped build a tool that let tradespeople capture a whole house in one visit. Using AI, the data was turned into quick findings shown on site, and a head start on the detailed renovation plan.

Senior Product Designer

2024–2026

Field survey app · Customer report

The problem
Subsidies can cover up to 70% of a retrofit, but only with a plan built from a site survey. Enter’s surveys were cheaper but often incomplete, and homeowners waited weeks or months for answers.
What I did
Rode along on surveys. Tested each idea in pencil or a prototype before building it. Designed the data capture tool, which was then used to generate an AI report while still on site.
The result
Shorter visits, more complete data, and homeowners selected their retrofits in their own kitchen.

01The problem

The funding existed. The data to claim it didn’t.

Every retrofit subsidy needs an iSFP, an official renovation roadmap an architect draws up from a survey of the house. Enter split that job in two: a tradesperson captures the building in one visit, and the architect builds the model and the plan from their data, back in the office.

That only works if one visit captures everything, well enough to build on. And the person on site is usually a Handwerker: a tradesperson who knows buildings inside out, has never opened a modelling tool, and has far less training than the architect.

02Research

Two hours in the house. A week to the answer.

I rode along on surveys, then followed the data back through the office. Three stages, two handovers, and information lost at both.

Stage 01 · The visit
2 hrs
Measuring, sketching and noting, all onto handwritten sheets.
Stage 02 · The desk
8 hrs
Someone else rekeys the sheets into the energy model, then chases what’s missing.
Stage 03 · The call
1 week
A colleague who has never seen the house presents the findings. It doubles as the sales call.

Ride-alongs: following the survey through the house, room by room.

Page three of six. All of it rekeyed at a desk later.

Enter wanted to automate almost everything after the visit. You can’t automate from handwriting.

03The first attempt

We put the form on a screen. Nothing changed.

Nothing expensive got built until something cheap had shown what it needed to do. So the first move was the same survey in Fastfield, an off-the-shelf forms tool, live within weeks. Typed and legible, but still a list of fields. Every window took a row of dropdowns for floor, wall, orientation and size, and by the 40th, people were rushing and rounding.

The data wasn’t poor because anyone was careless. We had made the accurate answer the slowest one to give.

04Paper prototype

Paper first. Then the engineering.

The real tool meant months of engineering, so I tested the idea for the price of an afternoon: a dimensioned floor plan from official building records, marked up in pencil on the tablet already in the van.

A riskier idea rode along with it. Instead of describing every window, define three or four window types once, then mark where each one sits. Each round went to the field first, then to the modelling team, to see whether they could build from what came back.

One sheet per floor, and one question: does this match the floor you’re standing on?

Marked up in the Files app on a tablet they already carried. Nothing was built to run this test.

The window types won

The idea I trusted least did the most. The slowest part of the survey nearly disappeared.

Completeness didn’t

05The survey app

A survey that follows the house, not the form.

Nobody surveys a house in order. You go down to the boiler, up to the loft, then back to the room someone was asleep in. So the survey became four tabs you can leave and come back to.

Almost nothing is free text. Heat sources, wall build-ups and glazing are presets, so the app carries the modelling knowledge and the person on site brings what they can see and measure. Before I designed a screen, engineering, the AI team and I agreed what a survey had to produce, down to what counts as a window. The same data fed the energy model, the homeowner’s report and the iSFP.

Before any of it was built, I tested a clickable prototype with 24 field workers across 13 states. Too few, and too spread out, to survey. So the tests ran as training sessions with the operations team: I watched people work, and changed the design between sessions.

One survey on two devices: a tablet for most visits, a phone when a tablet was one hand too many.

06The floor plan

Windows go on the plan, not in a form.

Find the house on a map, trace the heated part of it, and the floor plan exists. Then drag each window onto its wall, add its size and pick its type. Position comes from where you put it, not from a dropdown.

Official records had a plan for 90% of houses. For new builds and extensions the records hadn’t caught up with, six rough shapes and one tap keep the survey moving.

One window on the wall it sits in: room, size and type in one step.

07The estimate

The AI estimates. A professional corrects it.

The U-value, how much heat escapes through a wall or roof, is calculated live as details go in. Every figure stays editable, because someone looking into an opened-up wall knows more than a lookup table. That mattered more once Enter sold the tool to other firms, whose architects wanted the control.

An override more than 25% off the estimate is flagged, then accepted. Every override is logged: when the same estimate keeps getting corrected, that’s a ranked list of what the AI gets wrong.

The person on site sees what the model can’t.

08Data quality

Complete data, from houses and customers that don’t cooperate.

Some answers can’t be given on the day. Rather than let those gaps turn into guesses, the app makes someone own each one: the tradesperson on site, or the homeowner before anything is sent.

01 · On site

Skip a question, but say why.

The subsidy needs every critical field filled, so critical fields block the submit. But real houses have locked basements, and owners who don’t know when the windows went in. Force an answer and you get a guess that looks like a measurement. So the tradesperson can swipe to “Antwort nicht möglich”, which doesn’t clear the field. It asks why, from a fixed list.

02 · Before it’s sent

If the data isn’t there, the homeowner signs for it.

Before anything is sent, the homeowner goes through what was captured, in five steps on the tablet. Where they can’t provide something, like past energy bills, the app says plainly what that costs in accuracy, and they confirm it. The gap becomes their informed choice, not something the tradesperson missed.

Checked together, in the house, before anything leaves it.

09The customer report

The homeowner sees the findings before anyone leaves.

The moment the survey was sent, AI returned quick findings on the same tablet: where the house stands, what it could reach, and what each measure costs and saves. The tradesperson presented them there and then, instead of the homeowner waiting a week for the iSFP.

The same findings gave the architect a head start on the iSFP, the long, detailed plan written in the office afterwards. That mattered commercially. Enter earned its money arranging the retrofit work, and results that arrived days later on a follow-up call converted poorly. I designed the report from scratch, for a tradesperson rather than an architect to present.
