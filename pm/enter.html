Enter

A field app and an AI report that captured a whole house in one visit, and let the homeowner decide before anyone left. I led the design from the first ride-along to rollout.

Senior Product Designer

2024–2026

Engineering · AI team · Architects · Field operations

Field survey app · Customer report

The problem
Germany pays up to 70% towards a home retrofit, but only against an iSFP: a plan an architect builds from a site survey. Enter wanted to automate it, and the survey ran on paper.
What I did
Reframed the brief from digitising a form to capturing complete data, tested the idea on paper, agreed the data with engineering, and designed the field app and an AI report.
The result
Surveys 25% shorter, data 95% complete, and the homeowner’s choice of measures made in their kitchen, not on a call a week later.

01The business

The funding existed. The data to claim it didn’t.

Every retrofit subsidy needs an iSFP. Enter split that job in two: a tradesperson captures the house in one visit, and an architect builds the model and the plan back in the office. The plan was to automate most of the office half, and a model can’t be pointed at handwriting.

Enter made its money arranging the retrofit work, but its quotes arrived with the iSFP, days after the visit and from someone the homeowner had never met. Conversion was poor for reasons that had nothing to do with the advice.

The person on site is usually a Handwerker: a tradesperson who knows buildings inside out, has never opened a modelling tool, and has far less training than the architect.

Project outline

Each step bought what the next one needed to know.

Nothing expensive got built until the idea had been proven on something cheap. Five phases, from the first ride-along to rollout, each linking to its chapter.

03The stopgap

We put the form on a screen. Nothing changed.

The cheap move came first: the same survey in Fastfield, an off-the-shelf forms tool, live within weeks. It was typed and legible, but still a list of fields. Every window took a row of dropdowns, and by the 40th window people were rushing and rounding.

That changed the brief. The goal was no longer a digital form. It was a survey where the accurate answer was also the quick one, complete enough for a model to build on.

The data wasn’t poor because anyone was careless. We had made the accurate answer the slowest one to give.

04The paper test

Paper first. Then the engineering.

The real tool meant months of engineering, so I tested the idea for the price of an afternoon: a dimensioned floor plan from official building records, marked up in pencil on the tablet already in the van.

The test carried two bets. The footprint would be expensive to build, so it was the one worth proving first. The window types were a long shot: define three or four once, then mark where each one sits. Each round went to the field first, then to the modelling team, to check they could build from what came back.

One sheet per floor, and one question: does this match the floor you’re standing on?

Open the prototype sheet (PDF) ↗

Marked up in the Files app on a tablet they already carried. Nothing was built to run this test.

The footprint held

Close enough to correct on site, rather than measure from scratch.

The window types won

The idea I trusted least did the most. The slowest part of the survey nearly disappeared.

Completeness didn’t

Paper can invite a complete answer. It can’t enforce one, and that was the case for building.

05The survey app

We agreed the data first. The screens followed.

One survey fed the energy model, the AI, the architects and the report. So before designing any screen, I settled with engineering and the AI team what a survey produces, down to what a window is as a data object.

One rule for trade-offs: the model wins.

The person on site wanted to type less. The homeowner wanted to trust the number. The energy model needed every field, in a fixed structure, and can’t ask a follow-up question. Where they conflicted, the model won, because an incomplete iSFP means a blocked subsidy.

One window on the wall it sits in: room, size and type in one step.

Four sections, any order

Nobody surveys a house in order, so you can leave a section and come back. Submit stays locked until all four are done.

Presets, not free text

Heat sources, wall build-ups and glazing are picked, not typed. The app carries the modelling knowledge.

Windows on the plan

Trace the house on a map, then drag each window onto its wall. Records covered 90% of houses; six rough shapes cover the rest.

A declared way out

Critical fields block the submit. “Antwort nicht möglich” asks why, from a fixed list, so every gap arrives labelled.

Estimates you can overrule

U-values calculate live and stay editable. When people corrected the same default again and again, that was the model to fix.

The homeowner signs off

Nothing reaches the model until the homeowner has checked it, in five steps on the tablet.

06The customer report

The decision moved into the visit.

The moment the survey was sent, AI returned a report on the same tablet. The tradesperson walked the homeowner through it, and the homeowner picked the measures they wanted. Those choices went into the iSFP the architect wrote afterwards.

That was the commercial fix: the conversation now happened during the visit, not days later with a stranger. I designed the report from scratch, for a tradesperson rather than an architect to present.

07Rollout and results

Less time in the house. More of the house in the data.

Survey time fell by 25%, enough for about one more appointment a day, and the data reaching the energy model went from 80% to 95% complete. Homeowners now hear their findings during the visit. The certified iSFP still follows, but most of its inputs are imported rather than typed in again.

Before any code was written, we took a full prototype to 24 field workers across 13 states, run as on-site training with the operations team. That meant we could watch people work rather than ask them about it, and ship corrections between sessions.
